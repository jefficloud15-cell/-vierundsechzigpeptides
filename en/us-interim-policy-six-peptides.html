The US opens an interim path for compounding with six peptides

The US Department of Health published an enforcement-discretion policy for licensed pharmacies. It is not an FDA approval and can be revoked.

Vierundsechzig Newsroom · Published Nov 21, 2026Share: WhatsApp

On October 30, 2026, the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) published a policy in the Federal Register that changes, at least for now, the status of six peptides well known in research circles: BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, epitalon and Semax. It was signed by Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and came from the Office of the Secretary, not from FDA.

What happened

The policy is officially titled Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances. Put simply, HHS says it will not take enforcement action against state-licensed pharmacies, federal facilities and licensed physicians that compound with these six peptides, as long as they meet certain conditions.

The reported conditions are specific: every preparation needs a patient-specific prescription; the bulk substance must come from an FDA-registered facility with a valid certificate of analysis (CoA); ingredients must be traceable; and pharmacies must report serious adverse events and quality problems.

The context: a divided committee

The decision came three months after FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) voted in July to add these six peptides to the 503A list. According to AJMC, FDA career scientists had recommended the opposite for all seven peptides reviewed at that meeting.

HHS framed the policy as a way to confront the risks of black-market peptides: if there is a regulated route with traceability and testing, the argument goes, fewer people will turn to uncontrolled products.

Not everyone agrees. Partnership for Safe Medicines asked FDA to disregard the committee vote for lack of safety evidence. The Center for Science in the Public Interest warned in JAMA that the move weakens incentives to run clinical trials, and Public Citizen called for it to be rescinded.

What it means for research

The policy does not approve any peptide as a drug and does not establish safety or effectiveness. It applies only to US pharmacies filling prescriptions. For labs and research projects, the relevant point is different: the text puts traceability and the certificate of analysis at its center, the same criteria that separate serious research material from doubtful material.

Outside the United States, including Costa Rica, this policy changes nothing. Materials sold as RUO remain exclusively for laboratory research.

What comes next

The policy is interim: it stays in place while FDA completes rulemaking to add these substances to its 503A list, and HHS may revisit it if a safety signal or quality problem appears. The comment period closed two weeks after publication. The same committee is also due to review five more peptides before February 2027.

Frequently asked questions

Did FDA approve BPC-157 or TB-500?

No. The HHS policy is not an FDA approval and does not establish safety or effectiveness. It only states that, on an interim basis, there will be no action against 503A pharmacies that meet certain conditions.

Does this change anything in Costa Rica?

No. It is a US policy for pharmacies filling prescriptions. In Costa Rica, materials sold as RUO remain exclusively for laboratory research.
