Retatrutide vs. tirzepatide: differences between the triple and the dual agonist

Tirzepatide activates two hormone receptors, GIP and GLP-1. Retatrutide activates those two plus the glucagon receptor. That difference shapes their research profile: which receptors they hit, how strongly, and what each has shown in published studies.

In short
  • Tirzepatide is a dual agonist: it activates the GIP and GLP-1 receptors.
  • Retatrutide is a triple agonist: it activates GIP, GLP-1 and also the glucagon receptor.
  • Both are 39-amino-acid peptides with a fatty-acid chain that extends their half-life; about 6 days for retatrutide and 5 for tirzepatide.
  • Tirzepatide is already approved as a medicine in several countries; retatrutide is still in phase 3 clinical research.

First: GLP-1, GIP and glucagon

Both molecules mimic hormones the body already makes. Understanding those three hormones is understanding the difference:

  • GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1): released by the gut after eating. Research links it to glucose-dependent insulin release, slower gastric emptying and satiety.
  • GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide): the other major gut incretin. It also stimulates insulin and is studied for its role in fat tissue.
  • Glucagon: a pancreatic hormone that acts mainly on the liver. It mobilizes energy and is studied for its effect on energy expenditure and liver fat.

Tirzepatide: the dual agonist

Tirzepatide (code LY3298176) was the first molecule of its class to reach the market. It is a 39-amino-acid synthetic peptide whose sequence was designed to activate the GIP and GLP-1 receptors at the same time.

Its characterization describes it as an imbalanced and biased agonist: its action leans toward the GIP receptor, and at the GLP-1 receptor it favors certain internal signaling pathways (cAMP) over others. That combination is considered part of why it behaves differently from a pure GLP-1 agonist such as semaglutide.

It carries a 20-carbon fatty acid that makes it bind to albumin in the blood. That protects it from rapid clearance and gives it a half-life of about 5 days in studies.

Retatrutide: the triple agonist

Retatrutide (code LY3437943) goes one step further: besides GIP and GLP-1, it activates the glucagon receptor. That is why it is called a triple agonist or "triple G".

It is also a 39-amino-acid peptide with a C20 fatty acid, and its half-life in phase 1 studies was about 6 days. According to Eli Lilly, it is 8.9 times more potent than human GIP at its receptor, and less potent than native GLP-1 (2.5 times) and glucagon (2.9 times) at theirs: a profile with strong GIP action and balanced GLP-1 and glucagon action.

The key difference: the glucagon receptor

Activating the glucagon receptor can seem counterintuitive, because glucagon raises glucose. The research rationale is that, combined with GLP-1 and GIP (which keep glucose in check), the glucagon component adds other effects: higher energy expenditure and more use of fat as fuel, especially in the liver.

That showed up in a phase 2a study on fatty liver disease (MASLD): at 24 weeks, liver fat fell by an average of 82.4% in the highest-dose group, and 86% of those participants reached normal liver-fat levels, versus 0% with placebo.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between retatrutide and tirzepatide?

Tirzepatide activates two receptors (GIP and GLP-1); retatrutide activates three (GIP, GLP-1 and glucagon). The glucagon receptor is the key difference and is studied for its effect on energy expenditure and liver fat.

What does it mean that retatrutide is a triple agonist?

That it activates three different hormone receptors: GLP-1, GIP and glucagon. Tirzepatide is a dual agonist because it activates only the first two.

Which has the longer half-life?

In published studies, retatrutide's half-life is about 6 days and tirzepatide's about 5 days. Both use a fatty-acid chain that binds albumin to slow clearance.

Is retatrutide approved as a medicine?

No. As of November 2026 it is in phase 3 clinical research. Tirzepatide, by contrast, has been approved as a medicine since 2022. Research-grade compounds are not those medicines.

Why is tirzepatide listed as TR in the catalog?

TR is the abbreviation used in the Vierundsechzig Peptides catalog. The TR 15, 20, 30 and 40 mg listings are research-grade tirzepatide.
