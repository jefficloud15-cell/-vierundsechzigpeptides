Affinity, efficacy and potency

Affinity concerns the tendency to bind a receptor; efficacy concerns the ability to generate a response; potency relates to concentration needed for a defined effect. EC₅₀ commonly denotes the concentration reaching half the agonist's own maximum. High affinity does not guarantee high efficacy. [1] [2]

Full and partial agonism

A full agonist reaches the reference maximum of an assay. A partial agonist reaches a lower plateau even in the saturation region. This does not mean it occupies half the receptors. Nor does it imply lower potency in every case. Receptor density and signal amplification can change the observed classification. [1] [2]

Competitive antagonism

In a simple model a reversible competitive antagonist shifts an agonist curve toward higher concentrations without reducing its attainable maximum. Other mechanisms may lower the maximum. A partial agonist can reduce the response to a full agonist when both compete for the same receptors. Mechanism must be determined experimentally. [1] [2]

Inverse agonism and selectivity

Some receptors exhibit constitutive activity without ligand. An inverse agonist reduces that activity; a neutral antagonist does not do so on its own. Responses can also differ according to the intracellular pathway measured. Establishing functional selectivity requires appropriate controls and comparisons. [1] [2]

What assays establish

A binding assay indicates interaction, not necessarily activation. A functional assay measures a defined effect relative to controls. The illustrative curves normalize baseline response to zero and represent no real substance. Molecular activity does not establish clinical benefit.

Frequently asked questions

Is a partial agonist always less potent?

No. Potency and maximum response differ.

Does a neutral antagonist activate the receptor?

It does not itself produce the measured response.

Does classification depend on the assay?

Yes, on the system and response evaluated.
